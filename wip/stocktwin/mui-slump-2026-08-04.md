# The depth-averaged mu(I) rheology as a slump model for a stockpile face

Research dossier, 2026-08-04. Persisted before any implementation, per the standing rule that a plan
is built from resolved sources on disk and not from memory.

**Question asked.** StockTwin declares method 6, the depth-averaged mu(I) reclaim-face slump, and
states plainly in `docs/methods/06_mu-i-slump.md` that it is not implemented. Reclaim and dump faces
are today the pure angle-of-repose surface produced by the mass-conserving relaxation solver in
`bedblend/relax.py`, with the critical slope imposed at 37 degrees. What would implementing mu(I)
actually mean here, and is it worth it?

**Short answer, argued below.** The local mu(I) law and its depth-averaged form are real, well
sourced and quantitatively specified. Applied to a StockTwin face they change the geometry by less
than the model's own 2.5 m cell and 0.5 m voxel, they are provably ill-posed in exactly the state the
product spends most of its time in (a pile at rest, `I = 0`), and the one measurable prediction they
add over a fixed repose angle, the 1.6 degree static-to-dynamic hysteresis band, is already dwarfed
by a two-stage settle the engine implements today from field data. Recommendation: **do not
implement the slump solver. Delist method 6 and replace it with an implemented, honest frictional
hysteresis on the existing relaxation solver, which is a one-parameter change carrying the only
prediction that survives scrutiny.**

## 1. The local mu(I) friction law

### 1.1 Origin and mechanism

Dense granular flow is not Newtonian and it is not rate-independent Coulomb. The GDR MiDi collation
pooled experiments and discrete simulations across six geometries (plane shear, annular shear,
vertical chute, inclined plane, heap flow, rotating drum) and found that in every one of them the
ratio of shear stress to confining pressure collapses onto a single function of one dimensionless
group, the inertial number.

Mechanism, stated first because that is what matters. Take a grain sitting in a sheared assembly at
confining pressure $p$. Two clocks run. The **microscopic** clock is how long a grain takes to fall
into the next hole under the confining pressure, $t_{\text{micro}} = d\sqrt{\rho_*/p}$. The
**macroscopic** clock is how long the bulk takes to deform by order one, $t_{\text{macro}} =
1/\dot\gamma$. Their ratio is the inertial number. When $I$ is small the grains always have time to
find a stable seat, contacts endure, and the material behaves like a quasi-static soil at its
internal friction angle. When $I$ is large the grains are thrown across gaps before they settle,
contacts become collisional, and momentum transfer costs more friction. So friction **rises** with
$I$, saturating at a plateau, and the law is a friction coefficient that is a function of a single
number.

### 1.2 The equations

**Inertial number** (Gray and Edwards 2014, eq. 2.7; note the factor-2 convention, see the trap in
section 6.6):

$$I \;=\; \frac{2 d \lVert \mathbf{D} \rVert}{\sqrt{p/\rho_*}}$$

**Friction law** (Jop, Forterre and Pouliquen 2005 eq. as reproduced in Gray and Edwards 2014,
eq. 2.11; the requested form):

$$\mu(I) \;=\; \mu_s \;+\; \frac{\mu_2 - \mu_s}{\dfrac{I_0}{I} + 1}$$

which is algebraically identical to the equivalent common form $\mu(I) = \mu_s + (\mu_2 - \mu_s)\,
I/(I + I_0)$. It rises monotonically from $\mu_s$ at $I = 0$ to the plateau $\mu_2$ as $I \to
\infty$, crossing the midpoint at $I = I_0$.

**Tensorial closure** (Jop, Forterre and Pouliquen 2006; written as Gray and Edwards 2014, eqs.
2.3 to 2.6):

$$\boldsymbol{\sigma} = -p\mathbf{1} + \boldsymbol{\tau}, \qquad
\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mu(I)\,p\,\frac{\mathbf{D}}{\lVert \mathbf{D}\rVert}, \qquad
\mathbf{D} = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(\mathbf{L} + \mathbf{L}^{\mathsf T}\right), \qquad
\lVert \mathbf{D}\rVert = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\!\left(\mathbf{D}^2\right)}$$

with $\mathbf{L} = \nabla \mathbf{u}$, closed by incompressibility $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} = 0$. This
is a Bingham-like visco-plastic fluid whose yield stress is $\mu_s p$ and whose apparent viscosity is
$\eta = \mu(I)p / (2\lVert\mathbf{D}\rVert)$, which diverges as the strain rate goes to zero. That
divergence is the entire numerical difficulty of the model.

**Linear small-$I$ form** (da Cruz et al. 2005, eq. 14, two-dimensional discs), the earlier and more
primitive statement of the same physics:

$$\mu \;\simeq\; \mu_{\min} + b\,I, \qquad
\phi(I) \;\simeq\; \phi_{\max} - a\,I$$

valid in $10^{-2} \lesssim I \lesssim 0.2$, saturating above $I \approx 0.2$. The paper's own words:
"we measure an approximately linear decrease of the solid fraction from the maximum packing value,
and an approximately linear increase of the effective friction coefficient from the static internal
friction value."

## 2. The depth-averaged form

### 2.1 What depth-averaging gives that the local law does not

The local law is a three-dimensional visco-plastic closure. Solving it on a stockpile means a
free-surface Navier-Stokes solve with a strain-rate-singular viscosity over a 150 m by 150 m domain.
Gray and Edwards showed you do not have to. Under the shallowness assumption $\varepsilon = H/L \ll
1$, depth-integrating the mu(I) rheology yields two things:

1. **At leading order, the rheology collapses entirely into an effective basal friction**, and the
   resulting system is exactly the classical shallow-water avalanche model. Their own words: "To
   leading order, the rheology generates an effective basal friction, which is equivalent to a rough
   bed friction law... This is the only effect of the rheology on the flow, as the depth-averaged
   in-plane deviatoric stress gradient... does not contribute to the leading-order momentum balance."
   The Bagnold profile falls out, $\mu(I_\zeta) = \tan\zeta$ pins the inertial number to a constant
   set by the slope angle alone, and the depth-averaged velocity is Bagnold.

2. **At next order, one extra term appears**: a depth-averaged viscous-like term with a viscosity
   proportional to $h^{3/2}$ and an angle-dependent coefficient. This is the entire novelty of the
   2014 paper. It provides the high-wavenumber cutoff that the inviscid model lacks and can
   regularise segregation-induced fingering.

So: depth-averaging buys a **rate-dependent basal friction** in place of a constant Coulomb
coefficient, plus a **sub-metre smoothing term**. It does not buy a new face shape.

### 2.2 The resulting system

Gray and Edwards 2014, eqs. 4.13 to 4.16, dimensional, one-dimensional downslope form (their
$\chi = 1$ convention):

$$\frac{\partial h}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(h\bar u\right) = 0$$

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\left(h\bar u\right)
+ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\chi h \bar u^{2}\right)
+ \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2} g h^{2}\cos\zeta\right)
= h g S \;+\; \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\nu\, h^{3/2}\,\frac{\partial \bar u}{\partial x}\right)$$

$$S \;=\; \cos\zeta\left(\tan\zeta - \mu(Fr, h)\,\operatorname{sgn}(\bar u)\right) - \cos\zeta\,\frac{\partial b}{\partial x}$$

$$\boxed{\;\nu(\zeta) \;=\; \frac{2}{9}\,\frac{L\sqrt{g}}{\beta}\,
\frac{\sin\zeta}{\sqrt{\cos\zeta}}\,
\left(\frac{\tan\zeta_2 - \tan\zeta}{\tan\zeta - \tan\zeta_1}\right)\;}$$

with the effective basal friction expressed in Froude number and thickness (their eq. 2.8):

$$\mu(Fr, h) \;=\; \mu_1 + \frac{\mu_2 - \mu_1}{\dfrac{\beta h}{L\,Fr} + 1},
\qquad Fr = \frac{\lvert\bar u\rvert}{\sqrt{g h \cos\zeta}}$$

and the inertial number pinned by the slope (their eq. 3.18):

$$I_\zeta \;=\; I_0\,\frac{\tan\zeta - \tan\zeta_1}{\tan\zeta_2 - \tan\zeta}$$

**Additional symbols, with units**

| symbol | meaning | SI unit |
|---|---|---|
| $h(x,t)$ | flow thickness normal to the bed | m |
| $\bar u(x,t)$ | depth-averaged downslope velocity | m s<sup>-1</sup> |
| $b(x)$ | basal topography | m |
| $\zeta$ | slope inclination to the horizontal | rad (quoted in degrees) |
| $g$ | gravitational acceleration, 9.81 | m s<sup>-2</sup> |
| $S$ | non-dimensional net downslope acceleration source | dimensionless |
| $\nu$ | depth-averaged viscosity coefficient; the actual viscosity is $\nu h^{1/2}/2$ | m<sup>3/2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup> |
| $\chi = \overline{u^2}/\bar u^2$ | shape factor, $5/4$ for Bagnold, taken as 1 in practice | dimensionless |
| $Fr$ | Froude number | dimensionless |
| $\beta$ | slope of the empirical flow rule $Fr = \beta h/h_{\text{stop}} - \gamma$ | dimensionless |
| $\gamma$ | offset in the flow rule; zero for spheres, nonzero for angular grains | dimensionless |
| $L$ | length scale over which $\theta_{\text{stop}}$ crosses from $\zeta_2$ to $\zeta_1$; a **grain-and-bed-roughness** scale | m |
| $\zeta_1, \zeta_2, \zeta_3$ | the three critical angles (see section 3) | rad (degrees) |

**Deposit and start thickness** (Pouliquen 1999; Pouliquen and Forterre 2002 reciprocal fit, as
written in Edwards et al. 2019 eqs. 3.2, 3.5, 3.6):

$$\tan\theta_{\text{stop}}(h) = \tan\zeta_1 + \frac{\tan\zeta_2 - \tan\zeta_1}{1 + h/L},
\qquad
h_{\text{stop}}(\zeta) = L\left(\frac{\tan\zeta_2 - \tan\zeta_1}{\tan\zeta - \tan\zeta_1} - 1\right)$$

$$\tan\theta_{\text{start}}(h) = \tan\zeta_3 + \frac{\tan\zeta_2 - \tan\zeta_1}{1 + h/L},
\qquad
h_{\text{start}}(\zeta) = L\left(\frac{\tan\zeta_2 - \tan\zeta_1}{\tan\zeta - \tan\zeta_3} - 1\right)$$

### 2.3 The well-posedness caveat, which is fatal here

Barker, Schaeffer, Bohorquez and Gray (2015, JFM 779, 794 to 818) linearised the **incompressible**
mu(I) system about a base state and solved the resulting eigenvalue problem exactly in two
dimensions. The criterion, as restated in the open-access Barker and Gray 2017 (JFM 828, eq. 3.9):

$$C \;=\; 4\left(\frac{I\mu'}{\mu}\right)^{2} \;-\; 4\left(\frac{I\mu'}{\mu}\right)
\;+\; \mu^{2}\left(1 - \frac{I\mu'}{2\mu}\right)^{2},
\qquad \mu' = \frac{\mathrm{d}\mu}{\mathrm{d}I}$$

$$C > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{ill posed}, \qquad C \le 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{well posed}$$

Ill posed means Hadamard instability: the growth rate goes as $\lvert\boldsymbol{\kappa}\rvert^{2}$,
so grid-scale noise amplifies without bound as the mesh is refined and the numerical answer never
converges. Refining the grid makes it worse, faster, at shorter wavelength. This criterion was
reproduced numerically for this dossier and matches figure 1 of Barker and Gray 2017 for their
ballotini parameters (well-posed band $I \in [0.003, 0.28]$).

Two consequences, both of them decisive for a stockpile:

**(a) At $I = 0$, $C = \mu_s^{2} > 0$, always.** Substituting $I = 0$ kills the first two terms and
leaves $C(0) = \mu_s^2$. For $\mu_s = 0.38$, $C(0) = 0.144$. Barker and Gray say it in plain text:
"Purely static material when $I = 0$ and regions of large deformation are most likely to be ill
posed." **A stockpile between operations is $I = 0$ everywhere.** The rheology is not merely
inaccurate there, it is mathematically ill-posed there, unconditionally, for every parameter set.

**(b) The depth-averaged viscosity goes negative outside $[\zeta_1, \zeta_2]$.** From the boxed
$\nu(\zeta)$: as $\zeta \to \zeta_1^{+}$, $\nu \to +\infty$; at $\zeta = \zeta_2$, $\nu = 0$; for
$\zeta < \zeta_1$ or $\zeta > \zeta_2$, $\nu < 0$. Gray and Edwards state the consequence in their
own abstract: "Outside the range of angles where steady-uniform flows develop, the viscosity becomes
negative, which implies that the equations are ill-posed... The depth-averaged mu(I)-rheology
therefore cannot be used outside the valid range of angles without additional regularization."

A settled stockpile face sits **at or just below $\theta_{\text{stop}}$**, which for a thick deposit
tends to $\zeta_1$. That is precisely the singularity of $\nu$. With the glass-bead set ($\zeta_2 =
32.76^\circ$) a 37 degree face is **above $\zeta_2$**, so $\nu < 0$ outright. With the angular
carborundum set ($\zeta_1 = 31.1^\circ$, $\zeta_2 = 47.5^\circ$) a 37 degree face is inside the band,
which is the only reason this is arguable at all, and it is arguable only because the product's
imposed 37 degrees happens to fall between two angles measured on 0.3 mm silicon carbide.

Partial fixes exist and should be named honestly: Barker and Gray 2017 (JFM 828, 5 to 32,
doi:10.1017/jfm.2017.428) propose a partially regularised $\mu(I)$ curve that extends the well-posed
range; Heyman, Delannay, Tabuteau and Valance 2017 (JFM 830, 553 to 568) show that admitting
compressibility regularises the system, well posed provided a bulk friction $\mu_b > 1 - 7\mu/6$.
Neither restores well-posedness for genuinely static material without changing the model.

## 3. The key question: what does mu(I) predict that a fixed repose angle does not?

A stockpile face at rest is a static pile. mu(I) is a flow rheology. Four candidate predictions, each
checked against sources and against the product's discretisation (2.5 m cells, 0.5 m voxels, a
150 m by 150 m pad, peak heights of order 12.5 m in the shipped scenarios).

### 3.1 Prediction 1: hysteresis between the static and the drained angle

**This is real and it is the only one that survives.**

Daerr and Douady (1999, Nature 399, 241 to 243, doi:10.1038/20392) established the two-angle
phenomenology: a layer that has stopped will not restart until the slope is raised past a higher
angle. Pouliquen and Forterre (2002) folded it into a friction law with a multivalued static branch
at $Fr = 0$, a monotonically decreasing intermediate branch for $0 < Fr < \beta_*$, and the
monotonically increasing dynamic branch for $Fr \ge \beta_*$. Edwards et al. (2017, JFM 823, eq.
3.15) give the complete non-monotonic law, which is what you would actually code:

$$\mu(h, Fr) =
\begin{cases}
\mu_1 + \dfrac{\mu_2 - \mu_1}{1 + \dfrac{h}{L(Fr + \gamma)}} & Fr \ge \beta_* \\[3ex]
\left(\dfrac{Fr}{\beta_*}\right)^{\!\kappa}
\left[\mu_1 + \dfrac{\mu_2-\mu_1}{1 + \dfrac{h}{L(\beta_*+\gamma)}}
 - \mu_3 - \dfrac{\mu_2-\mu_1}{1 + h/L}\right]
+ \mu_3 + \dfrac{\mu_2-\mu_1}{1 + h/L} & 0 < Fr < \beta_* \\[3ex]
\min\!\left(\mu_3 + \dfrac{\mu_2-\mu_1}{1+h/L},\;\; \lvert \tan\zeta\,\mathbf{i} - \nabla h\rvert\right) & Fr = 0
\end{cases}$$

with $\kappa$ the intermediate-regime exponent (Edwards et al. use $\kappa = 1$; Pouliquen and
Forterre used $10^{-3}$, which Edwards et al. show is so extreme the boundary layer is invisible at
machine precision and is why their static layers would not stay put).

**Magnitude.** For a thick layer, $h/L \gg 1$, both correction terms vanish and

$$\theta_{\text{start}} - \theta_{\text{stop}} \;\longrightarrow\; \zeta_3 - \zeta_1$$

For the only angular material with a published triple, carborundum: $32.7^\circ - 31.1^\circ =
\mathbf{1.60}$ **degrees**.

**What 1.6 degrees is worth on a StockTwin face** (computed for this dossier, 12.5 m face,
2.5 m cells, 0.5 m voxels, repose 37 degrees):

| quantity | at 37.0 deg | at 38.6 deg | difference | in model units |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| horizontal run of a 12.5 m face | 16.59 m | 15.66 m | 0.93 m | **0.37 cells** |
| elevation drop across one 2.5 m cell | 1.884 m | 1.996 m | 0.112 m | **0.22 voxels** |
| conical pile volume on a fixed footprint | reference | | +5.94 % | material |

The face geometry moves by about a third of a cell and about a fifth of a voxel. The **volume**
effect is the only one that is not sub-discretisation, and it is a consequence of the angle, not of
the rheology: the product already exposes the repose angle as a control over a published 34 to 60
degree range, so a user can already produce a 5.94 percent capacity change with a 1.6 degree slider
move.

**And the product already implements a much larger hysteresis.** `bedblend/relax.py:431` `settle()`
holds the fresh truck-dumped heap at 2:1 (63.4 degrees) and then relaxes it to repose, sourced to
Young and Rogers (2021, Minerals 11, 636, doi:10.3390/min11060636, figure 11) from fleet-management
field data: "At the time of dumping, heaps maintain an approximate 2:1 slope... Over time the slope
decreases to that of the natural angle of repose." That is a **26 degree** emplacement-to-settled
hysteresis measured on real dumps. The mu(I) frictional hysteresis is 1.6 degrees measured on
0.3 mm carborundum. Adding the second on top of the first is adding a 6 percent correction to a term
already in the model, from a worse-matched material.

### 3.2 Prediction 2: the deposited angle depends on flow rate

**The sources say it does not, for the geometry StockTwin actually has.**

The strongest and most recent primary result is Ray and Khakhar (2025, JFM 1008,
doi:10.1017/jfm.2025.211), an experimental study of steady granular surface flow on a
**sidewall-free**, fully three-dimensional heap, which is a stockpile in miniature. Verbatim from the
abstract: "**The angle of inclination of the free surface of the heap is found to be nearly constant
for a ten-fold increase of the mass flow rate.** The scaled flowing layer thickness is found to vary
linearly with the scaled flow rate." The heap absorbs a rate change in the **thickness of the flowing
layer**, not in the surface angle.

Where a rate dependence does appear, the mechanism is sidewalls, not rheology. Taberlet et al.
(2003, PRL 91, 264301, doi:10.1103/PhysRevLett.91.264301) found "superstable heaps" standing far
above repose in a thin channel and showed with a depth-averaged Coulomb model that the excess angle
"scales as $h/W$" with $W$ the channel width, and that "grain size plays no part". A stockpile has no
sidewalls, so $W \to \infty$ and the effect vanishes. Jop, Forterre and Pouliquen (2005, JFM 541, 167
to 192, doi:10.1017/S0022112005005987) is the companion analysis, and the pile flow that Jop et al.
2006 used as the Nature paper's validation case was a pile **constrained between rough side walls**.

Direct simulation agrees on the small size of any residual: a molecular-dynamics study of heap
formation (arXiv:0711.2732) reports "increasing the rate of grain insertion R decreases the angle of
repose slightly. The change in $\theta_r$ is small, about a degree after increasing R three times",
consistent with Grasselli and Herrmann, and reports at most 0.3 degrees of fluctuation for changes in
grain mass.

Deposit **thickness** is likewise insensitive to the flow that made it. Edwards et al. (2019, JFM
875, doi:10.1017/jfm.2019.517) is the definitive statement: "Numerical simulations show that the
two-wave structure deposits layers that are approximately equal to $h_{\text{stop}}$ for all initial
thicknesses. This insensitivity to the initial conditions implies that $h_{\text{stop}}$ is a
universal quantity." They do add the caveat that "In general, however, the deposit thickness may
depend on the depth of the flowing layer that generated it", which is the honest residual, and it
concerns thickness rather than angle.

### 3.3 Prediction 3: a slumped face after a reclaim cut differs from the repose surface

**Transiently yes, at rest no, and the published continuum model is known to be wrong in exactly the
arrest phase.**

The right published analogue for a reclaim cut is **retrogressive failure**: remove support at the
toe of a static layer and an erosion wave propagates back upslope. Russell, Johnson, Edwards,
Viroulet, Rocha and Gray (2019, JFM 869, 313 to 340, doi:10.1017/jfm.2019.215) show that "a
relatively simple depth-averaged avalanche model with frictional hysteresis is sufficient to capture
a planar retrogressive failure", derive an exact travelling-wave solution, and locate the critical
point that sets the wave speed **in the intermediate frictional regime**, the branch for which "there
is no further experimental information" (Pouliquen and Forterre 2002) and for which "steady uniform
flows are unstable and so cannot be used to determine the friction law directly". So the one
quantity a reclaim-cut slump model would actually predict, how fast the failure eats back into the
face, is controlled by the least-constrained part of the whole theory.

The second analogue is **column collapse**, which is what a bucket-wheel or shovel cut into a face
resembles geometrically. Lagree, Staron and Popinet (2011, JFM 686, 378 to 408,
doi:10.1017/jfm.2011.335) implemented the full mu(I) rheology in a Navier-Stokes solver and compared
against contact-dynamics discrete simulations. Their finding about arrest is the one that matters
here, verbatim: "When the flow starts decelerating however, the continuum simulations slow down
earlier than discrete granular flows, and **systematically underestimate the run-out**, ie the front
final position. The error is larger for larger aspect ratios (reaching 10%) and coincides with the
final part of the granular deposit formed by a thin layer of a few grains in height, where the
assumption of a continuum is questionable." And in the conclusion: "Close to arrest however,
continuum simulations systematically underestimate the run-out in the case of large aspect ratios."

Restated for this product: the mu(I) continuum is validated in the **acceleration** phase and is
known to be biased in the **arrest** phase. StockTwin only cares about the arrested geometry.

**The transient is real but the product does not resolve time on that scale.** A slump on a 12.5 m
face at $\bar u \sim 3$ m s<sup>-1</sup> is over in about 5 seconds. The build loop places loads and
reclaims cuts; the artifact records geometry per event, not per second. There is no consumer for a
5 second transient.

### 3.4 Prediction 4: the viscous term reshapes the face

**No, and the paper that introduced the term says so.**

Gray and Edwards 2014, section 6, title and result: "Effect of viscosity on the shape of a granular
flow front", conclusion in their own abstract: "It is shown that a granular front propagating down a
rough inclined plane is **completely unaffected** by the rheology, but, discontinuities, which
naturally develop in inviscid roll-wave solutions, are smoothed out." And in the body: "For the large
majority of situations, the new depth-averaged mu(I)-rheology can be neglected, but when sharp
gradients in $\bar u$ develop, a boundary-layer forms in which the viscous terms play a significant
role."

Quantified for a stockpile. Scaling the carborundum $L$ by its measured $L/d = 1.4$ up to
run-of-mine grain sizes and evaluating $\nu(37^\circ)$ with $\beta = 0.63$:

| $d$ (m) | $L = 1.4d$ (m) | $\nu(37^\circ)$ (m<sup>3/2</sup> s<sup>-1</sup>) | flowing layer $h$ (m) | $\bar u$ (m/s) | viscous smoothing length $\ell$ (m) | in cells |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 0.070 | 0.117 | 0.5 | 2 | 0.21 | 0.08 |
| 0.20 | 0.280 | 0.468 | 1.0 | 3 | 0.61 | 0.24 |
| 0.50 | 0.700 | 1.170 | 2.0 | 4 | 1.32 | 0.53 |

with $\ell \sim \sqrt{\nu h^{1/2}\bar u /\left(g\cos\zeta\,(\mu_2-\mu_1)\right)}$ from balancing the
viscous term against the friction deficit, computed for this dossier. Even at half-metre rock the
smoothing length is **half a cell**. For the actual glass-bead reference set, $\nu(37^\circ)$ is
**negative** and the term is inadmissible.

### 3.5 What about the thickness dependence of $\theta_{\text{stop}}$?

This is the one place where a naive calculation looks like it favours implementation, and it should
be shown and then disarmed. Evaluating $\theta_{\text{stop}}(h)$ with the carborundum angles and
$L = 1.4d$:

| $d$ (m) | flowing layer $h$ (m) | $h/L$ | $\theta_{\text{stop}}$ | excess over $\zeta_1$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 0.5 | 7.1 | 33.55 deg | +2.45 |
| 0.20 | 1.0 | 3.6 | 35.37 deg | +4.27 |
| 0.20 | 2.0 | 7.1 | 33.55 deg | +2.45 |
| 0.20 | 12.5 | 44.6 | 31.55 deg | +0.45 |
| 0.50 | 2.0 | 2.9 | 36.12 deg | +5.02 |

Two to five degrees looks like a real effect, larger than the hysteresis band. It is not usable, for
two reasons. First, $L$ is a **fitted bed-roughness parameter**, measured only for sub-millimetre
grains on beds of glued sub-millimetre grains; $L = 1.4d$ is an extrapolation across three orders of
magnitude with zero supporting measurement, and the table's spread (2.45 to 5.02 degrees) is
generated entirely by the guess. Second, section 3.2's primary result contradicts it directly: the
sidewall-free heap angle is constant over a tenfold flow-rate change, so on the actual geometry the
flowing layer absorbs the rate and the angle does not move. The table measures the sensitivity to an
unmeasured parameter, not a prediction.

## 4. Is there published mu(I) work on stockpile or waste-dump geometry?

**Searched and not found.** The mu(I) literature is chute, plane-shear, silo, drum, column-collapse
and confined-heap. The applied mining literature does not use mu(I) for stockpiles or dumps. What it
does use:

- **Cellular automata on a voxel lattice**, which is what StockTwin already is. Ye, Hilden and
  Yahyaei (2022, Minerals Engineering 187, 107816, doi:10.1016/j.mineng.2022.107816, and part 2,
  2023, Minerals Engineering 200, 108156, doi:10.1016/j.mineng.2023.108156), from the JKMRC. Their
  stated reason is the one that governs here: a coarse ore stockpile holds roughly 220,000 particles
  per cubic metre and a large pile exceeds 200,000 cubic metres, so simulating an entire stockpile
  with DEM is impractical; continuum methods such as finite element analysis have been used for
  granular flows, and cellular automata are a simpler approximation with interaction rules per cell.
  Part 2 reports industrial validation of discharge and trajectory segregation. This is the closest
  published art to StockTwin and it does not carry a rheology.
- **Fleet-management-data geometry**, Young and Rogers 2021 (Minerals 11, 636,
  doi:10.3390/min11060636), already cited by `relax.py` for the two-stage settle.
- **Material Point Method with geotechnical constitutive models** for waste-rock-dump and tailings
  runout, driven by the Canadian Dam Association 2021 Tailings Dam Breach Analysis bulletin. These
  use Mohr-Coulomb and liquefaction models, not mu(I).
- **Continuum mu(I) in a mining-adjacent geometry** exists only for silo discharge (Staron, Lagree
  and Popinet; arXiv:1306.6279), which is a hopper, not a pile face.

Silence in the literature is weak evidence on its own. Combined with sections 2.3 and 3, the reading
is that the community has not applied mu(I) at this scale because the parameters are unmeasured
there, the static limit is ill-posed, and cheaper geometric rules reproduce the observable.

## 5. Verdict, and what should be built instead

### 5.1 Verdict

**Do not implement the depth-averaged mu(I) slump solver.** Building a shallow-water solver with a
non-monotonic friction law, a $h^{3/2}$ viscosity and a regularisation for the static limit, to move
a 12.5 m face by 0.37 of a cell and 0.22 of a voxel, while the paper that introduced the viscous term
states that the front shape is completely unaffected by it and the paper that solved the full
rheology states that continuum arrest underestimates runout, would be theatre. It would also change
the product's honesty profile for the worse: today method 6 is a named gap with a written explanation
of why a name without an engine is forbidden, which is a defensible position. A method that renders
identically to the current one, plus grid-dependent noise from an ill-posed static limit, would be
worse than the gap.

### 5.2 What to build instead: implemented frictional hysteresis, one parameter

There is one prediction in the whole body of work that is real, cheap, and not already in the model:
**a face that has come to rest can stand steeper than the angle it stops at, and will not move again
until it is disturbed or the angle is raised.** That is Daerr and Douady's two-angle phenomenology
and it is a one-line change to the existing solver, not a new solver.

Concretely, in `bedblend/relax.py`:

- add a second angle `hold_deg = repose_deg + delta_hyst_deg`, defaulting to
  `delta_hyst_deg = 1.6` from Edwards et al. 2017 table 1 ($\zeta_3 - \zeta_1$), exposed as a control
  and labelled as a carborundum-derived value applied to rock;
- `cascade()` **triggers** on a pair exceeding `hold_deg` but **relaxes to** `repose_deg`. Two
  thresholds, one solver. This is precisely the `theta_start` / `theta_stop` structure, discretised;
- the existing `settle()` two-stage call keeps the 63.4 degree emplacement stage unchanged; the
  hysteresis applies to the settled stage and to the post-reclaim relaxation;
- `count_over_repose` and `assert_stable` check against `repose_deg`, unchanged, so the invariant
  still says the surface can hold what it holds. `STABLE_TOL_DEG = 4.0` already exceeds 1.6, so the
  existing gate does not need to move and the hysteresis is inside the tolerance the product already
  accepts, which is itself an argument that this is a small effect honestly labelled.

Cost: tens of lines, one parameter, one new test (a face left at 38.6 degrees after a cut does not
move, and moves when the angle is raised past it, and lands at 37.0). Benefit: the reclaim face
after a cut is no longer identical to the deposition face, which is a real qualitative difference,
and the ledger sees a 5.9 percent volume difference on a fixed footprint. The doc for method 1 then
carries the hysteresis sources honestly, and `docs/methods/06_mu-i-slump.md` is rewritten as a
**delisting with reasons**, which is the same move method 7 already makes for the DEM lane.

### 5.3 The measurement that would settle it

If someone wanted to justify the full solver, the decisive experiment is cheap and is not a
laboratory one:

1. **Repeat terrestrial LiDAR or drone photogrammetry of one reclaim face**, at 0.05 to 0.10 m ground
   sample distance, three epochs: immediately before the cut, within 10 minutes after, and 12 to 24
   hours after with no intervening traffic. Fit a plane to the face in each epoch.
   - If $\theta_{\text{immediately after}} - \theta_{\text{next day}} > 2$ degrees and persists, a
     hysteresis band exists at this scale and is larger than the carborundum value, which would make
     the effect worth carrying explicitly. If it is under 1 degree, the effect is below the product's
     own cell size and the case is closed.
2. **Vary the reclaim rate by a factor of 5 to 10 across cuts on the same material and measure the
   resulting face angle.** Ray and Khakhar 2025 predicts no change. A measured change would be the
   first evidence that rate dependence matters at stockpile scale, and would then need a mechanism
   other than sidewalls.
3. **To parameterise mu(I) at all for this material**, measure $h_{\text{stop}}(\zeta)$ on a
   rough-bed inclined chute using the actual ROM size fraction. That gives $\zeta_1$, $\zeta_2$ and
   $L$ directly from the reciprocal fit, and $\zeta_3$ from the staircase procedure described in
   Edwards et al. 2019 section 1. Without those three numbers for rock, every mu(I) statement in this
   product would be a sub-millimetre glass-bead or carborundum number wearing a mining label.
4. **To check whether the theory is even admissible**, film a slump and estimate $I$ from the
   flowing-layer thickness and surface velocity. Estimates computed for this dossier (grain 0.05 to
   0.5 m, layer 0.5 to 2 m, surface speed 2 to 4 m s<sup>-1</sup>, $\rho_* = 2600$ kg m<sup>-3</sup>)
   put $I$ between 0.14 and 0.36, which is inside the well-posed band for the glass-bead parameter
   set ($I \in [0.019, 0.86]$ by the criterion in section 2.3) and near the upper neutral point for
   Barker and Gray's ballotini set ($I_{2N} \approx 0.28$). So the moving phase is arguably
   admissible and the resting phase is not, which is the whole problem.

## 6. Where it fails

Failure modes of the mu(I) approach in this specific product, stated so that nobody has to rediscover
them.

### 6.1 It is ill-posed for a pile at rest, unconditionally

$C(I = 0) = \mu_s^{2} > 0$ for every parameter set. Static material is the ill-posed limit of the
incompressible mu(I) rheology (Barker et al. 2015; restated in Barker and Gray 2017 section 3). A
StockTwin pile is at rest for essentially all of its modelled life. Any solve would be grid-dependent
by construction: refine the mesh and the noise grows faster and at shorter wavelength. Regularisation
(Barker and Gray 2017; Heyman et al. 2017 via compressibility) changes the model rather than fixing
it, and neither has been validated at metre scale on rock.

### 6.2 There are no mu(I) parameters for run-of-mine rock

Every published triple ($\mu_s$, $\mu_2$, $I_0$) in section 1.4 is for grains between 0.14 mm and
1 mm. StockTwin's material is 50 to 500 mm. The spread between the two best-characterised materials
is itself 10 to 15 degrees in the critical angles ($\zeta_1$: 20.9 for glass, 31.1 for carborundum;
$\zeta_2$: 32.76 for glass, 47.5 for carborundum). Choosing between them changes whether a 37 degree
face is inside or outside the admissible range of the depth-averaged theory. Any implementation would
be selecting a parameter set to make the answer admissible, which is fitting the physics to the
desired conclusion.

### 6.3 $L$ is unmeasurable at this scale and the answer is sensitive to it

$L$ sets $h_{\text{stop}}$, $\theta_{\text{stop}}(h)$, $I_0$ and $\nu$. It is a joint property of the
grains **and the bed roughness**, measured by a chute staircase procedure that has no stockpile
analogue. Section 3.5's table shows $\theta_{\text{stop}}$ moving 2.45 to 5.02 degrees purely from
the choice of $d$ in $L = 1.4d$.

### 6.4 The one prediction that survives is smaller than the discretisation

1.6 degrees of hysteresis moves a 12.5 m face's toe by 0.93 m against a 2.5 m cell (0.37 cells) and
changes the per-cell drop by 0.112 m against a 0.5 m voxel (0.22 voxels). The viscous smoothing
length is 0.21 to 1.32 m, that is 0.08 to 0.53 cells. Both are sub-discretisation.

### 6.5 It is smaller than the uncertainty in the quantity it corrects

`docs/methods/18_material.md` and `relax.py` both record that published handbook values for ore
repose angles span roughly 34 to 60 degrees, and that the value moves with particle size, moisture
and time since dumping. `STABLE_TOL_DEG = 4.0` in `relax.py` was set from exactly that reasoning:
"Asserting a surface to a micrometre against a quantity known to a few degrees is asserting the wrong
thing." A 1.6 degree correction sits inside a 26 degree published spread and inside the product's own
4 degree tolerance.

### 6.6 Implementation traps, if it is ever built anyway

- **The factor of two in $I$.** Gray and Edwards define $\mathbf{D} = \frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{L} +
  \mathbf{L}^{\mathsf T})$ and $I = 2d\lVert\mathbf{D}\rVert/\sqrt{p/\rho_*}$; Jop et al. 2006 use a
  different strain-rate normalisation, so "there is an extra factor of two in the definition of the
  non-dimensional inertial number" (Gray and Edwards 2014, after eq. 2.6). Copying $I_0 = 0.279$ into
  a solver that uses the other convention silently doubles or halves the inertial number.
- **$\rho_*$ is the intrinsic grain density, not the bulk.** Gray and Edwards state it explicitly:
  "in the definition (2.7) the intrinsic solid density of the grains is used rather than the partial
  density, which includes the interstitial pore space." StockTwin's `material.py` carries
  `rho_insitu`, `rho_loose` and `rho_placed`; the correct one here is closest to `rho_insitu`, and
  using `rho_loose` would misestimate $I$ by the swell factor, 30 to 45 percent for hard rock.
- **The intermediate-regime exponent $\kappa$ is a numerical choice with physical consequences.**
  Pouliquen and Forterre used $10^{-3}$; Edwards et al. 2017 use 1 and explain that the small value
  makes static layers fail to hold and finite masses run out too far. They recommend $\kappa$ between
  $10^{-1}$ and 1.
- **$\nu$ must be range-limited.** Gray and Edwards suggest clamping $\nu \in
  [\nu_{\min}, \nu_{\max}]$ with both non-negative for angles outside $[\zeta_1, \zeta_2]$, and add
  that "whether this provides a useful theoretical fix remains untested."
- **The shape factor.** $\chi = 5/4$ for a Bagnold profile, but essentially every avalanche model
  sets $\chi = 1$ because non-unity values change the characteristic structure and break grain-free
  regions (Edwards et al. 2019 section 2, citing Hogg and Pritchard 2004). A pad with bare cells is
  full of grain-free regions.

### 6.7 What this dossier does not settle

- Whether a genuinely **wet or cohesive** face behaves differently in a way mu(I) could capture. It
  could not: mu(I) is a dry rheology. Cohesive extensions exist (Abramian et al.; "Collapse of a
  cohesive granular column") and were not researched here.
- Whether **segregation-induced fingering** on the reclaim face is a real phenomenon at stockpile
  scale. This is the one application where the depth-averaged mu(I) viscous term has a genuine job
  (Woodhouse et al. 2012 showed the coupled size-segregation model is ill-posed without it), and it
  would couple to method 4. It is a different question from face geometry and was out of scope.
